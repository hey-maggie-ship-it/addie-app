What is body doubling, and why it works

Ankora Guides · 5 min read

There's a strange, reliable trick a lot of people discover by accident: a task you've avoided for a week suddenly gets done in twenty minutes the moment another person is in the room. They aren't helping. They might be doing something else entirely. But their presence flips a switch, and you can finally start. That's body doubling, and it's one of the most effective and least understood tools for getting unstuck.

What body doubling actually is

Body doubling is doing a task in the presence of another person, who acts as an anchor while you work. The double doesn't coach you, supervise you, or do the task with you. They just exist nearby, maybe folding laundry or answering their own email, while you do the thing you couldn't face alone. It works in person, and it works over video with someone you barely interact with. The presence is the point, not the participation.

Why it works (it's not about being watched)

First, the myth: body doubling is not surveillance. It doesn't work because someone is checking on you or grading your output. Most doubles aren't even paying attention. Here's what's actually going on:

  • It externalizes the start. A gentle, ambient sense that someone is there makes beginning feel less optional, without the threat that a boss or a hard deadline carries.
  • It anchors your attention. An ADHD brain drifts. A second person in the space is a soft tether that keeps pulling you back to the room and the task in front of you.
  • It borrows a calmer state. Focus is a little contagious. Sitting beside someone who's on-task helps you settle into on-task too, the same way it's easier to go for a run with a partner than alone.
  • It shrinks the loneliness of the task. Much of avoidance is really dread, and dread is heavier when you carry it alone. A witness, even a silent one, makes the task feel survivable.

The magic ingredient is low stakes. A body double is presence without pressure. That's exactly why it slips past the wall of dread that a demanding, watching authority figure would only make taller.

The forms it can take

  • A friend or partner on the other end of the couch, each doing your own thing.
  • A coworker at the next desk, which is half of why an office "worked" for some of us and going solo quietly stopped working.
  • A virtual coworking session or focus room, where people sign on, name a goal, and work in parallel on mute.
  • A quick video call with a friend where you both say what you're doing and then go quiet and do it.
  • A cafe, where ambient human presence does a lighter version of the same job.

How to actually do it

You get far more out of a double with a little structure:

  • Say your intention at the start. Out loud: "I'm going to draft this for 25 minutes." Naming it makes it real and gives the session an edge.
  • Keep the double low-maintenance. They shouldn't have to manage you. The whole value is that showing up is easy for both of you.
  • Check in at the end. One sentence about how it went closes the loop, and oddly, it makes you want to come back and do it again.
  • Timebox it. A defined block beats an open-ended "let's work," which quietly becomes "let's chat."

When there's no one to sit with you

The catch with body doubling is availability. The task hits at 9pm, or on a Tuesday when everyone's busy, and the whole thing depends on a person being free at the exact moment you're stuck.
